Artifact 1 · AI Lab · AIML-500 Machine Learning Fundamentals Complete

Digital Safety AI: building a cybersecurity awareness chatbot

Introduction

Digital assistants powered by generative AI are becoming a common way for people to get guidance and practical advice in everyday language. In this AI Lab, our team used generative AI to design and build Digital Safety AI, a chatbot that teaches everyday internet users the basics of staying safe online.

Description

Digital Safety AI is a custom GPT that explains common online threats, helps users spot phishing and scams, gives advice on creating strong passwords, and promotes responsible technology use. It was built on ChatGPT with custom instructions and user context, then tested against realistic questions.

The bot is deliberately limited to education and awareness. It refuses to give hacking or illegal-access instructions and steers users away from unethical cyber activity.

Objective

Help users build a foundational knowledge of cybersecurity and safe online behavior so they can reduce their risk from common cyber threats, using clear language that beginners can follow.

Process

We first practiced with a base large language model, running the same prompt (“Explain common signs of phishing emails”) several times. Every answer covered the core warning signs, but detail, structure and examples varied between runs. That showed us a general model was useful but too generic, so we built a custom GPT and organized the project around the five stages of design thinking:

  1. EmpathizeIdentified the problems users face online: spotting phishing emails, weak passwords, low awareness of security practices, and unsafe use of public Wi-Fi.
  2. DefineFramed the core problem: most internet users lack the knowledge to recognize and protect themselves from cyber threats.
  3. IdeateCompared four concepts: an online safety adviser, a cybersecurity awareness chatbot, a password-security helper, and a computer-based safety education aid. We chose the awareness chatbot because it best served our goal.
  4. PrototypeConfigured the custom GPT with an objective, capabilities and limits, and added scenario context such as advice for connecting to public Wi-Fi.
  5. TestRan test conversations and refined the instructions based on what we saw.

Test scenarios

“What are some clues to look for in phishing e-mails?”

The bot listed suspicious links, urgent language, unknown senders and requests for personal or confidential information.

“What should I do to make a good password?”

The bot recommended longer passwords, mixing letters, numbers and symbols, and avoiding guessable words or personal details.

Challenges and how we solved them

Challenge

Some answers used technical jargon that beginners found hard to follow.

Solution

Rewrote the instructions to ask for simple language and plain explanations.

Challenge

Some answers gave far more information than the question needed.

Solution

Refined the prompts to ask for brief, focused answers.

Artifact 2 · Group presentation · AIML-500 Machine Learning Fundamentals Complete

Fraud detection in milliseconds: how machine learning and deep learning protect card payments

Introduction

Every time someone taps a card, a model decides in a fraction of a second whether the payment looks safe. For our Group 3 presentation, Smarter Business, One Prediction at a Time, our team explained how machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) power three business use cases: predictive analytics, fraud detection, and recommendations. I researched and presented use case 2, fraud detection.

Description

The full deck is a 15-slide, roughly 15-minute talk for a business audience. It moves from core concepts (how ML and DL differ) to a six-step model lifecycle, three real-world use cases, guidance on choosing the right tool, and the risks businesses face.

My section covers fraud detection, where models protect money in real time. It explains the scale of the problem using published figures from Visa and American Express, then walks through what happens in the two milliseconds after a card is tapped and why companies combine classic ML with deep learning instead of picking one.

Objective

Show a non-technical business audience how ML and DL work together to stop fraud at very large scale, and make a technical idea (sequence models reading purchase history) easy to picture. The wider goal of the presentation was to help business leaders choose between ML and DL based on their data and the question they are asking.

Process
  1. Divided the topicThe team split the talk into four speaking parts. I took the featured example, fraud detection, which connects the concepts from earlier slides to a high-stakes real business problem.
  2. Researched credible sourcesGathered figures from published sources: Visa's report of about $40 billion in fraudulent payments blocked in 2023 (Dludla, 2024), and American Express's use of deep learning to score more than 8 billion transactions a year within a 2 ms limit (Ashley, n.d.).
  3. Turned numbers into a storyDesigned a slide of four large figures so the scale of the problem lands immediately: $40B blocked, 8B+ transactions, 2 ms per decision, and up to 6% better accuracy in specific segments after adding deep learning.
  4. Explained the decision flowBuilt a flow diagram that follows one card payment: the signals collected, a gradient-boosted tree model scoring them as a table, an LSTM network reading the order of recent purchases, and the outcome (approve, ask to verify, or decline).
  5. Wrote speaker notes and rehearsedScripted about 1.5 minutes per slide in plain language, with a hand-off from the churn example ("Predicting churn can wait a month. Fraud has to be caught in milliseconds.") so the talk flowed between speakers.
  6. Cited in APA 7Added source lines on each slide and a shared reference list so every statistic can be checked.
